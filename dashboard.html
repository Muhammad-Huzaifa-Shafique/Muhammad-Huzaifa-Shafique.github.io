Gapminder Interactive Dashboard

An animated Quarto report, built with R and Plotly for a Data Visualization course, on how life expectancy and income changed in 142 countries between 1952 and 2007. The explorer below is a small JavaScript version made for this site. It uses the 1952 and 2007 rows of the same dataset.

Jump to the explorerOriginal animated reportSource code

Relevant to: exploratory data analysis (EDA), data visualization (R, Plotly, Quarto, JavaScript and SVG), log scales, choosing the right aggregate (population-weighted against simple average), explaining results to non-specialists, reproducible reports.

Explore the data

Each dot is a country. It sits further right the higher its income per person (log scale), and higher up the longer people live. The area of a dot is the country's population. Drag the slider from 1952 to 2007, or pick a continent to highlight it.

Choosing the average

The report weights continent averages by population, so that a continent's number describes the typical person, not the typical country. Switch the average below and watch the Americas and Asia move.

Life expectancy by continent, 1952 and 2007

19522007

  • The United States held about 46% of the Americas' people in 1952, so a simple average of the continent's countries (53.3 years) sits about 7 years below the population-weighted one (60.2).
  • For Asia the effect runs the other way. China and India were large and had short lives in 1952, so weighting lowers the figure from 46.3 to 42.9 years.
  • Weighted by population, Asia gained the most, 26.5 years (42.9 to 69.4). Africa gained 15.8 (38.8 to 54.6) and is still the lowest.
  • The gap between the highest and lowest continent narrowed from 30.4 years to 26.5. Oceania is highest and Africa lowest in both years.

What the data shows

  • The median country's life expectancy rose from 45.1 to 71.9 years, and its median GDP per person from $1,969 to $6,124.
  • In 1952, 82 of the 142 countries had a life expectancy under 50 years and 5 had 70 or more. By 2007 those counts were 19 and 83.
  • Only two countries had a lower life expectancy in 2007 than in 1952: Swaziland (41.4 to 39.6 years) and Zimbabwe (48.5 to 43.5).
  • Income and life expectancy rise together across countries. The data shows that they move together, not that one causes the other.

Limits. The data stops in 2007 and is sampled every five years, and this page loads only the first and last of the twelve years. Each value is a country-level average, so it hides differences inside a country. Oceania has only two countries (Australia and New Zealand). Population on this page is rounded to 0.1 million.

Next step, not built yet. Fit a simple model, for example life expectancy from log income, year and continent. Train it on 1952 to 1997 and test it on 2002 and 2007, so it is judged on years it has not seen.

The original report

The report itself is one Quarto file that rebuilds everything with R. It has two animations: one shows every country (income against life expectancy, colored by continent), the other shows population-weighted life expectancy by continent. Each comes with the question it answers, a plain-language explanation, a summary table and a limitations section.

  1. Data. gapminder_dataset.csv: 1,704 rows, 142 countries every five years from 1952 to 2007, from Gapminder.
  2. Choices. Income on a log scale, so a few very rich countries do not squeeze everyone else into one corner. Continent averages weighted by population.
  3. Build. quarto render Animation_data.qmd installs the R packages it needs (plotly, scales, knitr) and writes the finished HTML.
